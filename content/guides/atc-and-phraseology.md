# ATC and radio phraseology for GeoFS pilots

The structure of a good radio call, the standard phrases you will hear most often, and how to fly with community controllers.

## Why phraseology matters

Real-world radio frequencies are crowded, and every transmission blocks everyone else. Standard phraseology exists so that each call is short, unambiguous, and in a predictable order. The same is true when GeoFS pilots fly with community controllers: a clear call gets a clear answer, and a frequency where everyone uses the same phrases runs much more smoothly.

You do not need to be perfect. Controllers on community servers are used to pilots who are learning. But knowing the basic structure will make you sound confident and help you understand what you are being told.

## Finding live ATC

Airports with active community air traffic control from ATCThing show a live green indicator on [the radar](/radar). Select it to join the controller's Discord. You can also open **Airports** in the radar's control dock. This airport activity board ranks airports by traffic and lists the airports that are currently staffed by controllers.

Before you call a controller for the first time, listen for a minute. You will learn which frequency positions are open, how busy it is, and what style the controller prefers.

## The structure of a call

Most calls follow the same pattern: **who you are calling, who you are, where you are, and what you want**.

> *"London Ground, Speedbird one-one-seven, stand two-three, request push and start."*

- **Who you are calling**: the station, such as *London Ground*.
- **Who you are**: your callsign, using the airline's telephony name. See [Callsigns explained](/guides/callsigns-explained).
- **Where you are**: your position, such as a stand, a holding point, or an altitude.
- **What you want**: the request.

When you respond to an instruction, reverse it: read back the instruction first, then give your callsign.

> Controller: *"Speedbird one-one-seven, climb flight level two-four-zero."*
>
> Pilot: *"Climb flight level two-four-zero, Speedbird one-one-seven."*

## Numbers and letters

Numbers are read digit by digit, with a few exceptions:

- *Nine* is pronounced *niner* to avoid confusion with the German *nein*.
- Altitudes in thousands use *thousand*: 5,000 feet is *five thousand*.
- Flight levels are read as three digits: FL350 is *flight level three-five-zero*.
- Headings are always three digits: 090 is *heading zero-niner-zero*.
- Frequencies use *decimal*: 121.5 is *one-two-one decimal five*.

Letters use the phonetic alphabet: Alfa, Bravo, Charlie, Delta, Echo, Foxtrot, Golf, Hotel, India, Juliett, Kilo, Lima, Mike, November, Oscar, Papa, Quebec, Romeo, Sierra, Tango, Uniform, Victor, Whiskey, X-ray, Yankee, Zulu.

## What to read back

You must read back anything that changes where the aircraft goes or what it is allowed to do:

- Runway instructions: *line up*, *cleared for take-off*, *cleared to land*, *hold short*.
- Altitude, heading, and speed instructions.
- Route clearances and direct-to instructions.
- Squawk codes and frequency changes.

Traffic information and weather do not need to be read back in full. A simple acknowledgement with your callsign is enough.

## A typical flight, call by call

### Clearance delivery

> Pilot: *"Heathrow Delivery, Speedbird one-one-seven, Airbus A320, stand two-three, information Alfa, request clearance to Edinburgh."*
>
> Controller: *"Speedbird one-one-seven, cleared to Edinburgh, departure as filed, climb altitude six thousand feet, squawk four-five-two-one."*
>
> Pilot: *"Cleared to Edinburgh, as filed, climb six thousand feet, squawk four-five-two-one, Speedbird one-one-seven."*

*Information Alfa* is the ATIS letter: it tells the controller you have listened to the latest airport information. Set your assigned squawk in the RadarThing userscript panel so it appears on the radar; [Squawk codes explained](/guides/squawk-codes) covers what the codes mean.

### Ground

> Pilot: *"Heathrow Ground, Speedbird one-one-seven, stand two-three, request push and start."*
>
> Controller: *"Speedbird one-one-seven, push and start approved, facing east."*

Then, when ready to taxi:

> Controller: *"Speedbird one-one-seven, taxi holding point runway two-seven-right via Alfa, Bravo."*

Follow the taxi route on the [ground chart](/guides/airport-charts).

### Tower

> Controller: *"Speedbird one-one-seven, runway two-seven-right, cleared for take-off, wind two-six-zero degrees, eight knots."*
>
> Pilot: *"Cleared for take-off, runway two-seven-right, Speedbird one-one-seven."*

### Departure and en route

> Pilot: *"London Control, Speedbird one-one-seven, passing three thousand five hundred, climbing six thousand."*

Always report your current altitude and your cleared altitude when you first contact a new radar controller.

### Arrival and approach

> Controller: *"Speedbird one-one-seven, descend flight level one-two-zero, expect ILS runway two-four."*
>
> Controller: *"Speedbird one-one-seven, turn left heading two-seven-zero, descend three thousand feet, cleared ILS approach runway two-four."*

### Landing

> Controller: *"Speedbird one-one-seven, runway two-four, cleared to land, wind two-three-zero degrees, ten knots."*

After landing, vacate the runway, stop if unsure, and wait for taxi instructions to your stand.

## Useful phrases

- **Roger**: I have received your message. It is *not* a yes and not a readback.
- **Wilco**: I will comply.
- **Affirm**: yes. (*Affirmative* is avoided because it sounds like *negative* on a poor line.)
- **Negative**: no.
- **Say again**: please repeat.
- **Standby**: wait, I will call you back.
- **Unable**: I cannot comply with that instruction.
- **Go around**: abandon the approach and climb.

## Etiquette with community controllers

1. **Fly what you were told.** If you cannot, say *unable* rather than ignoring the instruction.
2. **Keep calls short.** Save conversation for text channels.
3. **Tell the controller if you are new.** Most controllers will happily slow down and help.
4. **Leave cleanly.** If you have to stop mid-flight, tell the controller before you disconnect, then end your flight in the RadarThing panel.
5. **Respect events.** Busy events have extra procedures. Read the event brief before you join.

Following these habits makes you easy to work with, and makes your flights better for everyone sharing the sky. Phraseology on RadarThing is for simulator use and is simplified; real-world phraseology varies between countries.
